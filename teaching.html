Teaching

Learning to Ask “How Do We Know?”

We’re surrounded by information, and the line between fact and fiction keeps getting blurrier. More than anything, I want my students to leave as critical consumers of information: people who can find evidence, weigh it, and make good decisions with it. That matters in the clinic, and it matters everywhere else too.

What that looks like in class

“Google it.”

When the class gets stuck, I often say exactly that. Then we look at the results together and talk about which sources to trust, and why.

Headline vs. study

Each year, students compare a news story about a hearing-science finding with the research paper it’s based on. What got lost? What got exaggerated? What’s missing?

Concept Checks, not multiple choice

Students apply ideas to real clinical scenarios instead of memorizing answers.

The whole person

I check in on how students are actually doing, adjust when the semester gets heavy, and remind them that their well-being matters as much as their grades. Take breaks, go outside, have hobbies. I practice what I preach.

My classes are interactive and occasionally exuberant. I walk (sometimes run) around the room, prompting students to explain their thinking out loud.

Training the next generation of clinicians

Director

Project REACH-SLP

A $1.1 million training program funded by the U.S. Department of Education. It prepares master’s students to become speech-language pathologists who work with children and young people, ages 3 to 22, who have high-intensity needs. The program focuses on recruiting and mentoring future clinicians from backgrounds underrepresented in the field, and on preparing them to serve children from many cultures and languages, including in high-poverty schools.

Co-director

Project iPASS

A $1.25 million program, also funded by the U.S. Department of Education, that trains future speech-language pathologists alongside adapted physical education teachers.
